## DL-2026-09-28-01 — Read the regulator's own text, not a summary

**Areas:** Business and leadership; Education and domain knowledge

**Tags:** `regulation`, `primary-sources`, `verification`

### What I learned

A secondary write-up of a regulatory clause can silently drop the limit that decides the question. A children's
product-safety standard allows rewards only as low-stakes motivational devices "not related to … academic achievement, or
outcomes outside of the learning task". The summaries I first relied on reported it as a plain ban on rewarding
achievement. Re-reading the primary text showed the clause is ambiguous: it may forbid any reward that scales with correct
answers, or only rewards that carry value beyond the task (grades, rankings, status).

### What I did

While designing a pupil reward system, I stated the clause as a ban, was challenged, went back to the regulator's own
page, quoted the sentence in full, and set out the two readings for a legal reviewer instead of choosing one.

### Evidence or example

The design that survived is safe under both readings: rewards are earned on true completion of a task (every mistake
reviewed and corrected), not scaled by the score. The question of scaling by score went to legal review with the exact
quotation and URL.

### Why it matters

A design built on a summary can forbid something the rule allows, or allow something it forbids. When a regulatory
clause decides product behaviour, the quotation and its source are the evidence; the summary is only a pointer.

### Open questions

How can a team flag, at review time, any compliance claim that cites a secondary source instead of the primary text?

### Next step

Quote the primary text, with its URL and retrieval date, in every requirement that rests on a regulation.
